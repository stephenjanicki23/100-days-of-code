Cageside Replay

Four fights the simulation actually produced, drawn two ways — once in a path tracer and once live in the browser. Both renderers are fed the directive stream on the right and nothing else — one row per event, each naming a prebuilt clip, a camera hint and the reaction on the receiving fighter. It has no access to the fight engine, no attributes, no ability scores. If it can draw a fight from this, so can a game engine.

Path-traced offline — the same fight, a slower lens

Rendered frame by frame in a path tracer, driven by the very same directive stream the live viewer below reads. Nothing about the fight changed — only the renderer on the other end. This buys what no integrated GPU can do in real time: light that actually scatters through skin, shadows with soft edges because the lights have size, and bounce from the canvas filling the shadow side of a body. It took twenty-nine minutes of CPU to make and costs your laptop nothing to watch.

Before and after — the animation layer, rewritten

The same fight, the same three and a half seconds, the same seed — only the choreography changed. On the left a clip returns a pose: all nineteen joints, so a fighter throwing a punch cannot also be stepping, and the body snaps between discrete states. On the right a clip claims only the parts of the body it needs and the rest is left to the stance underneath; the feet stay planted until the leg can no longer reach and then step; the pair circle instead of facing along a fixed axis; and the fighters carry their own habits — one presses forward on heavier legs, the other moves and resets more.

The right-hand side also registers being hit, which the left never did. Reactions were selected by damage thresholds of 3 and 8 against a scale where a landed strike carries about 0.4 to 2.5 — so every clean strike mapped to no reaction at all, and the impact camera never fired once in a whole fight. Nothing errored; there was only an absence.

The result

Directive stream — the renderer's only input

    Nothing here is scripted

    The fights were simulated by the engine from the seed showcase-2026. Same seed, same fights, forever — including which judge scored which round the other way.

    Events describe, they never direct

    The engine emits what happened; a static registry resolves it into a clip and a camera hint. No camera angle or timing curve ever enters the event contract, which is what lets one stream feed text, statistics and this.

    Two renderers, one contract

    The path tracer and the browser share no code — different language, different renderer, different machine. They agree because they read the same directives. An engine client would be a third.

    The cage is a contest

    Position used to be a sine curve around the centre mark. Now the man with more forward pressure walks the other onto the fence, and the one who lives at range works an angle off it. Watch Styles make fights: a brawler puts a karate fighter's back to the mesh for half the fight.

    Ten decisions a second

    The engine used to resolve exchanges: one fighter acted, the other waited, and a fifteen-minute fight held about eighty decisions. It now ticks at 0.1s with each fighter on an independent clock, so the two drift against each other and a man can be caught mid-combination. These four fights carry 886, 737, 1797 and 634 events — three to four times what they held before.

    Weight class is a real input

    It was not one. Measured over two hundred fights, flyweights and heavyweights both threw 6.9 significant strikes a minute and finished at the same rate. Now every pacing number is keyed by class: flyweights throw 12.3 a minute and win 70% by decision, heavyweights throw 7.6 and finish 71% inside the distance.

    A trait that was never there

    Every movement trait came through one normalisation written for a 0–200 attribute scale the generator never produced. Against the real 1–98 range it clamped the median fighter to zero: most of the roster never circled, never tired, never feinted. Like the reaction thresholds before it, nothing errored — there was only an absence.
